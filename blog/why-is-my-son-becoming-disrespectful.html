How can I help my teenage son who is disrespectful and stubborn?

Why a teenage boy becomes disrespectful and stubborn, what makes it worse, what helps, and when a mentor or camp can help.

By Banio Luiji Nobert, Founder of Boys to Men Mentorship Camp

Begin by knowing that part of what you are seeing is normal, and part of it you can influence. Neither part is a reason to give up on him.

Why a good boy suddenly pushes back

Researchers who study teenagers explain that boys push for independence faster than their ability to manage themselves grows. The part of the brain that handles feelings develops ahead of the part that handles self-control. So he wants less control while still struggling to control himself, and conflict follows. A child welfare guide from Australia adds that the teenage brain also finds it harder at times to see another person's point of view, including yours.

The same researchers say these conflicts help a family renegotiate authority, as long as the relationship stays warm.

What tends to make it worse

Psychologists at the Child Mind Institute warn that relying on punishment and shame does not lead to lasting progress, and that when most exchanges between a parent and a teenager are negative, defiance tends to grow. Another study found that teenagers accept warnings far more easily from parents they see living by their own values.

What tends to help

Say your expectations clearly before there is a quarrel, not in the middle of one. Stay calm, even when he is not. Give a consequence you are ready to enforce, and then enforce it. Give him a little room to cool off. Ask questions such as what he thinks should be done, rather than only giving orders. UNICEF advises listening without rushing to judge and asking how you can help.

Find moments with him that have nothing to do with correction. A meal, a walk or a job done side by side rebuilds the trust that discipline depends on.

When to ask for more help

If your son is fighting or bullying others, drinking, stealing, running away, or seems deeply sad or withdrawn, speak to a school counsellor, a teacher or a doctor. Good parents seek help early.

Where a camp fits

Sometimes a boy who will not take correction from his father will take it from a mentor ten years older, or from boys his own age who say the same thing. At the Boys to Men Mentorship Camp, he works on anger, respect, healing and brotherhood among other boys and with several mentors. It is not a punishment and it is not a quick fix. It is a fresh start, and a doorway to better conversations at home.
