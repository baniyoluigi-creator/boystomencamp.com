A Question Parents Ask Us

Who can mentor my son when he does not listen to me?

How to find a mentor for a boy who has stopped listening to his parents, what good mentors look like, and how the Boys to Men Mentorship Camp helps.

By Banio Luiji Nobert, Founder of Boys to Men Mentorship Camp

A son who stops listening to a parent is often still listening. He is just listening to someone else.

The village that used to help

Not long ago a boy in Uganda was raised by a whole community. Uncles, grandfathers, neighbours and elders corrected him, taught him and watched over him. Migration, city life and the pressure to earn a living have thinned that circle. Many boys now grow up with parents who leave early and return late, and with no elder close by.

A 2021 report from the AfriChild Centre at Makerere University found that nearly one in five children in Uganda live in homes where the father is absent. Ugandan family advocates have also estimated, from interviews and not from a national count, that only about five in every hundred fathers are both present and emotionally close to their children. What the village once did without being asked, parents must now do on purpose.

What a good mentor looks like

Look for a person with a life you would be glad for your son to copy. Look for someone whose behaviour stays the same whether or not anyone is watching, who is a few steps ahead of your son, who shares the values you want him to hold and who is willing to correct him with respect. Look for someone who works openly, and who keeps you informed. It may be an uncle, a teacher, a church or mosque leader or a family friend.

What mentoring can and cannot do

A review of 70 youth mentoring studies found that mentoring helps, though the effect is moderate. Programmes did better when they served more boys, used more male mentors and drew on mentors with a background in the helping professions. Mentoring is a strong support, but it does not replace you.

How the camp helps

At the Boys to Men Mentorship Camp, a pool of more than twenty mentors share the work, and each boy meets several of them during the week. Patricia Kokora and Eduardo Fortunato Atibuni will call your family, and your son may call either of them whenever he wishes. Guest speakers have included Dr Joel Aita, who shares the Chinese bamboo tree story, and Letaru Deborah and Dr Okidi Lawrence, who speak on health, nutrition and a responsible lifestyle.

Parents have their own session, so that you and the mentors can say the same things in the same way. When your son hears a message from you, from a mentor and from other boys, it is far harder to ignore.
