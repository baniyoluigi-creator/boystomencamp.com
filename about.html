Our Story

Boys to Men Mentorship Camp began as a response to a simple, urgent observation: too many boys are moving into manhood without the mentorship, guidance, or safe space they need to do it well. Scroll down to read the full story, as told by our founder.

Read the full story ↓

As told by Banio Luiji Nobert, Founder

Where this began

Growing up, I watched my family break. I was about four or five years old when my father and my mother had a heated disagreement. In anger, he tried to cut my mother with a panga (machete), but luckily she escaped. He then cut their mattresses into several pieces. He also cut down the crops we had growing around our house. To this day I do not know what the disagreement was, but whatever upset him that day must have been extreme. For a small child who loved his mother, it was very hard to erase this from my mind. The next day, my parents separated, and that is when our family fell apart.

Until a few years ago, I judged my father and wished I could sit down with him to ask what had happened. As I grew older, I began to ask a different question. Every child is born innocent, loving, happy and emotionally whole. What if my father was not simply a bad man, but also a victim of the society that raised him? People say he was a brilliant man. He did very well in school, he was funny, and he was full of life. Like most of us, he was flawed too. Then he got lost in alcohol. I do not know every struggle he carried. I do know that he grew up where alcohol was brewed and sold to pay school fees, so he may have met it very early, and some habits are very hard to break.

By the time I was going to Primary Two, I had lost both of my parents. I lived in various homes afterwards, four or five of them, until I came to the house of my mother’s elder brother, who raised me and cared for me greatly. When he passed on suddenly in my Senior Two, his wife still kept me. To this day, she is the amazing woman I call Mum.

Yet the fatherhood gap somehow remained. Whatever I became as a man, especially the little good others may sometimes feel I carry, I learned in small pieces from different men who dared to step in, and from others I only watched from a distance. I would not wish that for any young person. I wish they had a father who provides for them financially, supports them emotionally and mentors them. A boy may not have a father who prepares him for life, but opportunities still exist, and this camp is one of them. It helps fill the gaps left in single mother households like the one I grew up in, or by busy fathers. Over time, I realised that I had two choices. I could keep blaming, or I could help build a world where both men and women are more emotionally grounded and better prepared for life.

What school did not teach us

My own life experiences after school added to this. Years later I looked back and saw that I had not made the kind of progress I expected by that time. Like many other people, there were so many things I had never been taught. I then concluded that education must be more than academic. Family upbringing, mentorship and exposure must fill the remaining gaps. Young people also need to understand their minds, build good habits, grow in emotional intelligence, manage anger, understand money, grow a career, speak and lead well, build networks, and start and grow businesses.

So we created the MEP Masterclass, through which adults learn how to become better parents and grow in their careers, rising from one position or salary scale to the next. They learn how to start and grow a business, especially as they prepare for life beyond formal employment. They learn how to live lives filled with achievable goals they remain committed to, build the right networks, and speak and lead better, among other things.

Most of the adults who went through this life readiness programme told us the same thing. Again and again they said, “If only I had learned these things when I was younger, there are mistakes I would have avoided and my progress would be far ahead.” They believed they would have built better relationships, been better parents, managed money better and grown faster in their careers. Then they added, “I wish my child could go through this, so that they do not have to feel that adulthood is a scam like we did.” That created the urgency to begin a programme for young people. We run it separately for boys and for girls so that each group can concentrate. Boys to Men is the programme for boys.

What society expects of a man

As boys grow up, society asks them a question, not always in words. Are you ready? The answer shows up in jobs, business, networks, money and habits. Our work is to prepare them. We see six expectations that society places on a man.

He is expected to provide value through a career or business, living within his purpose and contributing to others. He is expected to earn an honest living and to live with both independence and interdependence, providing for himself, his family and those in his care. This needs resources, and it needs the right mindset. He is expected to lead, himself first and then his family, and in Africa often people at work and in business too, so he needs a positive influence wherever he goes. He is expected to use words well. Children and women need affirmation from the fathers and husbands in their lives. A man who cannot say what he needs and feels may turn to violence, withdraw from the world, or break down. His silence can wound as deeply as harsh words. He is expected to build a family and raise children who carry his good character. And he is expected to protect his family and society, never to be the one who is violent or who engages in domestic violence. He is a safe space for others.

Maybe, if my father had had this opportunity, he would have managed his anger better. Maybe, if my mother had had such an opportunity, she would have known how to avoid the disagreement in the first place. Should other young people go through the same? Should their children tomorrow have the same experiences as I did? No!

How the camp answers

During the seven days, each boy meets more than ten different mentors. Hearing different things from different people fills the gaps left by formal education and parenting. I wanted every boy to have in front of him the structure I never had, so that he can learn about the things that matter, from money and career to behaviour, relationships and marriage. Our aim is boys who grow into men who are responsible, ambitious, grounded, confident, secure and driven by impact.

Where we are today

What began with the hope of touching one life has grown. Hundreds of boys have been through the camp, and tens of thousands of people have been reached through outreach in schools, on radio and in other places. Beyond the camp, our company, Potential Unexplored Limited, helps people become leaders who can lead themselves and then others. It also helps organisations become their most productive selves through leadership training, coaching, mentorship and inspiring team building that teaches. In all of it we help people discover their potential, develop it and deploy it, so they can reach the peak of what is possible for them.

Every boy is born with promise. Our job is to help him become a man who is ready.

This is how we build bridges to manhood in Africa.

V

Our Vision

To raise a generation of responsible, confident, and purpose-driven men who positively influence their families, communities, and society.

M

Our Mission

To provide transformational mentorship experiences that help boys discover their potential, develop their capabilities, and deploy their strengths for meaningful impact.

Our Values

What we will not compromise on

R

Respect

Every boy is treated with dignity, never shamed or singled out.

C

Calm

We coach, we do not command. Results come from a calm, respectful approach.

I

Integrity

We do what we say, especially with parents trusting us with their sons.

C

Community

A boy's growth does not end when camp ends, ours does not either.

Our Philosophy

Discover. Develop. Deploy.

Every boy carries potential. Potential alone does not create results. It has to move through three deliberate stages to become real progress.

1

Discover

Who he is: identity, purpose, self-awareness, and the potential already within him.

2

Develop

Who he can become: personal mastery, practical mastery, and people mastery.

3

Deploy

What he does with it: leading himself, leading others, and creating real impact.
